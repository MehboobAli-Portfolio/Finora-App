# Tax Basics

## What are capital gains taxes?
Capital gains tax is the tax you pay on profit from selling an investment. There are two types: Short-term capital gains apply to investments held for less than one year and are taxed at your regular income tax rate (10-37%). Long-term capital gains apply to investments held for more than one year and are taxed at lower rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income). This is why long-term investing is so tax-efficient — by holding investments for over a year, you can significantly reduce your tax bill. For example, if you earn $10,000 profit on a stock held for 13 months, you might pay only 15% ($1,500) instead of 24% ($2,400) if you had sold before the one-year mark.

## What is tax-loss harvesting?
Tax-loss harvesting is a strategy where you sell investments that have lost value to offset the taxes on your gains. For example, if you made $5,000 profit on Stock A and lost $3,000 on Stock B, you can sell Stock B to realize the loss, reducing your taxable gains to $2,000. You can also deduct up to $3,000 in net capital losses against your regular income each year. After selling, you can reinvest in a similar (but not identical) investment to maintain your portfolio allocation. Be aware of the wash sale rule — you cannot buy back a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale.

## What tax deductions should I know about?
Common tax deductions that reduce your taxable income include: Mortgage interest (on loans up to $750,000). State and local taxes (SALT) up to $10,000. Charitable contributions. Medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of adjusted gross income. Student loan interest (up to $2,500 per year). Home office deduction if you are self-employed. Business expenses for freelancers and entrepreneurs. Retirement account contributions (Traditional IRA, 401k). Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions. You should itemize deductions if their total exceeds the standard deduction ($14,600 for single filers, $29,200 for married filing jointly in 2024).

## How do retirement accounts reduce taxes?
Traditional 401k and Traditional IRA contributions are tax-deductible, meaning they reduce your taxable income in the year you contribute. If you earn $60,000 and contribute $10,000 to a Traditional 401k, you are only taxed on $50,000. The money grows tax-deferred (no taxes on gains while invested) and you pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement. Roth IRA and Roth 401k contributions are made with after-tax money (no immediate tax break), but all future growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free. If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement, Roth accounts are generally better. If you need the tax break now, Traditional accounts are better.

## What is a Health Savings Account (HSA)?
An HSA is a triple-tax-advantaged account available to people with high-deductible health plans. Contributions are tax-deductible (like a Traditional IRA), growth is tax-free (like a Roth IRA), and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. This makes the HSA the most tax-efficient account available. The 2024 contribution limits are $4,150 for individuals and $8,300 for families. A powerful strategy is to pay medical expenses out of pocket now, let your HSA investments grow, and reimburse yourself years later for maximum compound growth.

## Do I need to pay taxes on cryptocurrency?
Yes. The IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, not currency. Every time you sell crypto, trade one crypto for another, or use crypto to buy goods or services, it is a taxable event. If you held the crypto for more than a year, profits are taxed at long-term capital gains rates. If less than a year, they are taxed as short-term gains at your ordinary income rate. Simply buying crypto with cash and holding it is not taxable. Mining and staking rewards are taxed as ordinary income at their fair market value when received. Keep detailed records of all crypto transactions for tax reporting.
