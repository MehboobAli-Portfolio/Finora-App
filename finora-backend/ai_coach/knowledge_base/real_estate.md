# Real Estate

## Should I rent or buy a home?
The rent vs buy decision depends on several factors. Buying makes sense if you plan to stay in the same area for at least 5-7 years (to recoup closing costs), have a stable income, can afford a 10-20% down payment without depleting your emergency fund, and the monthly mortgage payment (including taxes and insurance) is not more than 28% of your gross income. Renting makes sense if you value flexibility and may need to relocate, cannot afford a down payment yet, live in an extremely expensive market where buying is disproportionately costly, or prefer not to deal with maintenance and repair costs. There is no universal right answer — both renting and buying can be financially smart depending on your situation.

## How much should I save for a down payment?
The traditional recommendation is 20% of the home price to avoid Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI). For a $300,000 home, that is $60,000. However, many loan programs allow lower down payments: FHA loans require as little as 3.5%, conventional loans can go as low as 3%, and VA loans (for veterans) require 0% down. The trade-off with a lower down payment is that you will pay PMI ($50-200 per month extra), have a larger mortgage, and pay more interest over the life of the loan. A balanced approach is to aim for at least 10% down while maintaining your emergency fund and not depleting your retirement savings.

## What are the hidden costs of buying a home?
Beyond the mortgage payment, homeownership comes with significant additional costs that renters do not face: Property taxes (typically 1-2% of home value per year). Homeowners insurance ($1,000-3,000 per year). Maintenance and repairs (budget 1-2% of home value per year). HOA fees if applicable ($200-500 per month in some areas). Closing costs when purchasing (2-5% of the home price). Utilities that may be included in rent but not in ownership. Lawn care, pest control, and seasonal maintenance. When comparing the cost of renting vs buying, make sure to include ALL these expenses, not just the mortgage payment.

## What is a mortgage and how does it work?
A mortgage is a loan used to purchase real estate. You borrow money from a bank, buy the home, and repay the loan over 15 or 30 years with interest. Common types include: Fixed-rate mortgage (interest rate stays the same for the entire loan term — most popular and predictable). Adjustable-rate mortgage or ARM (lower initial rate that adjusts after a fixed period — riskier but can save money if you sell before the adjustment). The total cost of a mortgage is much higher than the purchase price. For a $300,000 home with a 7% 30-year fixed mortgage, you would pay approximately $418,000 in interest alone, making the total cost about $718,000.

## Is real estate a good investment?
Real estate can be an excellent investment for building wealth, but it requires significant capital and management. Advantages include: Appreciation (home values historically increase 3-5% per year), rental income (passive cash flow), tax benefits (mortgage interest deduction, depreciation), and leverage (you control a $300,000 asset with only $60,000 down). Disadvantages include: Illiquidity (cannot sell quickly like stocks), concentration risk (one property in one location), maintenance costs, tenant management headaches, and vacancy risk. Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) offer a way to invest in real estate without buying physical property — they trade like stocks and pay dividends from rental income.

## How do I know if I can afford a house?
Follow the 28/36 rule: Your monthly housing payment (mortgage, taxes, insurance) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total monthly debt payments (housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards) should not exceed 36% of your gross income. For example, if your gross monthly income is $6,000, your maximum housing payment should be $1,680 and your total debt payments should not exceed $2,160. Also ensure you have at least 3-6 months of expenses saved as an emergency fund AFTER making your down payment.
