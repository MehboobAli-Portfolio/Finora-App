# Investing Basics

## What is investing and why should I invest?
Investing means putting your money into assets like stocks, bonds, or real estate with the expectation that they will grow in value over time. The main reason to invest is that keeping money in a savings account typically earns 0.5-5% interest, while inflation averages 2-3% per year, meaning your purchasing power actually decreases over time. Historically, the stock market has returned an average of 8-10% per year over the long term. By investing consistently, you harness the power of compound growth to build serious wealth over 10, 20, or 30 years.

## What are stocks?
A stock represents a small ownership share in a publicly traded company. When you buy a stock, you literally become a partial owner of that business. If the company grows and earns more profits, the stock price typically increases, and you make money when you sell. Some stocks also pay dividends, which are regular cash payments to shareholders. Stocks are considered higher risk than bonds but historically offer higher returns. Popular stocks include Apple (AAPL), Tesla (TSLA), Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN), and Google (GOOG).

## What are ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)?
An ETF is a basket of many stocks or bonds bundled into a single investment that you can buy and sell like a regular stock. Instead of picking individual companies, you buy the entire basket. For example, the SPY ETF contains all 500 companies in the S&P 500 index, giving you instant diversification. ETFs are considered one of the best investments for beginners because they spread your risk across many companies, have very low fees (typically 0.03-0.20% per year), and require no research into individual stocks. Other popular ETFs include QQQ (top tech companies) and VTI (entire US stock market).

## What is Dollar Cost Averaging (DCA)?
Dollar Cost Averaging means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals (weekly, biweekly, or monthly) regardless of what the market is doing. For example, investing $500 every month into an S&P 500 ETF. When prices are high, your $500 buys fewer shares. When prices are low, your $500 buys more shares. Over time, this averages out your purchase price and removes the stress of trying to "time the market." DCA is one of the most recommended strategies for long-term investors because it is simple, disciplined, and historically effective.

## What is compound interest and how does it work?
Compound interest is when your investment earnings generate their own earnings. Instead of earning interest only on your original investment, you earn interest on your interest. For example, if you invest $10,000 at 8% annual return: After Year 1 you have $10,800. After Year 2 you have $11,664 (not $11,600, because the $800 from Year 1 also earned 8%). After 10 years you have $21,589. After 20 years you have $46,610. After 30 years you have $100,627. The key insight is that compound growth accelerates over time — the earlier you start investing, the more powerful it becomes. This is why starting to invest in your 20s is dramatically more impactful than starting in your 40s.

## What is diversification?
Diversification means spreading your investments across different types of assets so that if one investment performs badly, others can offset the loss. A well-diversified portfolio might include US stocks (50%), international stocks (20%), bonds (20%), and alternative assets like real estate or gold (10%). The idea is to never put all your eggs in one basket. If you only own Tesla stock and Tesla drops 40%, your entire portfolio drops 40%. But if Tesla is only 5% of your diversified portfolio, a 40% drop in Tesla only affects your total portfolio by 2%.

## What are bonds?
Bonds are essentially loans you make to governments or corporations. When you buy a bond, the issuer promises to pay you back the full amount (face value) on a specific date, plus regular interest payments along the way. Government bonds (like US Treasury bonds) are considered very safe. Corporate bonds pay higher interest but carry more risk. Bonds are generally less volatile than stocks and are used to add stability to a portfolio. A common recommendation is to hold a percentage of bonds equal to your age — so a 30-year-old might hold 30% bonds and 70% stocks.

## What are mutual funds?
A mutual fund pools money from many investors and uses it to buy a diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other securities. Mutual funds are managed by professional fund managers who decide which investments to buy and sell. The main difference between mutual funds and ETFs is that mutual funds are priced once daily and often have higher management fees (0.5-2% per year). Index mutual funds, which simply track a market index like the S&P 500, tend to have much lower fees and have been shown to outperform most actively managed funds over the long term.

## How much money do I need to start investing?
You can start investing with as little as $1. Many modern brokerages offer fractional shares, meaning you can buy a portion of an expensive stock. For example, if Amazon stock costs $180 per share, you can buy $10 worth and own a fraction of a share. The most important thing is to start early and invest consistently, even if the amounts are small. Investing $100 per month starting at age 25 at an 8% return will grow to approximately $349,000 by age 65. The same $100 per month starting at age 35 only grows to approximately $149,000 — less than half.

## What is a portfolio and how should I build one?
A portfolio is your complete collection of investments. A balanced portfolio for a moderate-risk investor might look like this: 60% Stocks (mix of US and international), 25% Bonds (government and corporate), 10% Real Estate (REITs), 5% Alternatives (gold, crypto). For aggressive investors willing to take more risk for potentially higher returns: 80% Stocks, 10% Crypto, 5% Bonds, 5% Alternatives. For conservative investors who prioritize safety: 40% Stocks, 40% Bonds, 15% Cash/Savings, 5% Real Estate. Your portfolio should match your risk tolerance, time horizon, and financial goals.

## Should I invest or pay off debt first?
This depends on the interest rate of your debt. If your debt has a high interest rate (above 7-8%, like credit cards at 15-25%), pay it off first because no investment consistently beats those rates. If your debt has a low interest rate (below 5%, like some mortgages or subsidized student loans), you can invest simultaneously because the stock market historically returns 8-10%. A balanced approach is to make minimum payments on low-interest debt while investing your surplus, and aggressively pay down any high-interest debt before investing heavily.
