# Debt Management

## What is the debt avalanche method?
The debt avalanche method is the mathematically optimal strategy for paying off multiple debts. You list all your debts from highest interest rate to lowest. You make minimum payments on all debts, then put every extra dollar toward the debt with the highest interest rate. Once that debt is paid off, you move to the next highest rate. For example, if you have a credit card at 22% APR, a car loan at 6%, and a student loan at 4.5%, you would aggressively pay off the credit card first while making minimum payments on the others. This method saves you the most money in total interest paid over time.

## What is the debt snowball method?
The debt snowball method, popularized by Dave Ramsey, focuses on paying off debts from smallest balance to largest, regardless of interest rate. You make minimum payments on everything, then throw all extra money at the smallest debt. When that is paid off, you roll that payment into the next smallest debt. While this costs more in total interest than the avalanche method, it provides quick psychological wins that keep you motivated. If you struggle with motivation and need to see progress fast, the snowball method can be very effective.

## How do I get out of credit card debt?
Credit card debt is one of the most dangerous forms of debt because interest rates typically range from 15-25% APR. Here is a step-by-step plan: First, stop using your credit cards immediately. Switch to cash or debit only. Second, list all your credit card balances and interest rates. Third, call your credit card companies and ask for a lower interest rate — this works more often than people think. Fourth, consider a balance transfer to a 0% APR card if you qualify, giving you 12-18 months of interest-free payments. Fifth, use the avalanche method to pay down the highest rate card first. Sixth, once paid off, keep the cards open but use them only for small recurring bills to maintain your credit score.

## Should I pay off debt or save for emergencies first?
Build a small emergency fund of $1,000 first before aggressively paying off debt. This prevents you from going deeper into debt when unexpected expenses arise (car breakdown, medical bill). Once you have this mini emergency fund, attack your high-interest debt aggressively. After all high-interest debt is eliminated, build your full emergency fund of 3-6 months of expenses.

## How do I deal with student loan debt?
Student loan debt strategies depend on your loan type. For federal student loans, explore income-driven repayment plans (IDR) that cap payments at 10-20% of discretionary income. Check if you qualify for Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) if you work for a government or nonprofit organization. For private student loans, consider refinancing if you can get a lower interest rate, but be aware you lose federal protections. Generally, if your student loan interest rate is below 5%, it is considered low-interest debt and you can invest simultaneously rather than paying it off aggressively.

## What is good debt vs bad debt?
Good debt is borrowing that helps build wealth or increase your earning potential. Examples include mortgages (real estate typically appreciates in value), student loans (higher education leads to higher lifetime earnings), and business loans (used to generate income). Bad debt is borrowing for things that lose value or do not generate returns. Examples include credit card debt for consumer purchases, car loans for expensive vehicles beyond your means, and payday loans with predatory interest rates. The goal is to minimize bad debt and use good debt strategically.

## How do I calculate my debt-to-income ratio?
Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is calculated by dividing your total monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income, then multiplying by 100. For example, if you pay $500 in rent, $300 in car payment, and $200 in student loan payments ($1,000 total), and your gross income is $4,000, your DTI is 25%. Lenders generally consider a DTI below 36% as healthy, and below 28% as excellent. A DTI above 50% is a red flag that indicates financial stress.
