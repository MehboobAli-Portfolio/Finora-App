# Budgeting Strategies

## What is the 50/30/20 budgeting rule?
The 50/30/20 rule is one of the most popular budgeting frameworks. It works by dividing your after-tax income into three categories: 50% goes to Needs (rent, groceries, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments), 30% goes to Wants (dining out, entertainment, shopping, subscriptions, hobbies), and 20% goes to Savings and Debt Repayment (emergency fund, investments, extra debt payments). For example, if your monthly income is $3,000, you would allocate $1,500 to needs, $900 to wants, and $600 to savings. This rule is a great starting point if you have never budgeted before because it is simple and flexible.

## What is zero-based budgeting?
Zero-based budgeting means every single dollar of your income is assigned a job before the month begins. Your income minus all planned expenses should equal exactly zero. This does not mean you spend everything — savings and investments count as "jobs" for your money. For example, if you earn $4,000, you might assign $1,200 to rent, $400 to food, $200 to transport, $300 to entertainment, $600 to savings, $500 to investments, and $800 to other bills. The total equals $4,000, leaving zero unassigned. This method gives you maximum control and awareness of where every dollar goes.

## What is the envelope budgeting method?
The envelope method is a cash-based budgeting system where you physically put cash into labeled envelopes for each spending category. When an envelope is empty, you stop spending in that category for the month. For example, you might have envelopes for Groceries ($400), Dining Out ($150), Entertainment ($100), and Gas ($120). The digital version of this is essentially what Finora does — tracking your spending by category and alerting you when you approach your limits. This method is excellent for people who tend to overspend because it creates a hard, visible limit.

## How do I create a monthly budget?
Step 1: Calculate your total monthly after-tax income from all sources. Step 2: List all your fixed expenses (rent, utilities, loan payments, subscriptions). Step 3: Review your past 3 months of spending to understand your variable expenses (food, transport, shopping). Step 4: Decide how much you want to save or invest each month (aim for at least 20%). Step 5: Assign dollar amounts to each category, making sure total spending plus savings equals your income. Step 6: Track your actual spending daily using Finora and compare against your plan.

## How do I stick to my budget?
The most effective strategies for sticking to a budget are: Review your spending daily using Finora to stay aware. Use the 24-hour rule for non-essential purchases over $50 — wait a full day before buying. Automate your savings by transferring money to savings the day you get paid, not at the end of the month. Cancel unused subscriptions — most people waste $50-100 per month on services they do not use. Cook meals at home — dining out is typically 3-5x more expensive. Set specific, motivating goals in Finora so you have a reason to save.

## What should I do if I overspend my budget?
If you go over budget in a month, do not panic. First, identify which categories caused the overspending by checking your Finora spending analytics. Second, look for one-time expenses versus recurring overspending. If it was a one-time event (car repair, medical bill), adjust and move on. If it is a pattern (consistently overspending on dining out), reduce your budget in wants categories or find ways to cut back. The key is to not give up entirely — even imperfect budgeting is better than no budgeting at all.

## What is the pay-yourself-first budgeting approach?
Pay-yourself-first means the moment you receive your paycheck, you immediately transfer a fixed percentage (ideally 20% or more) into savings or investments before paying any other bills. The rest of your income is then used for expenses. This approach works because it prioritizes your financial future and forces you to live on what remains, rather than saving whatever is left over at the end of the month, which is usually nothing.
